Coffee Cost Percentage: What Should It Be?

Published April 2026  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  7 min read  ·  CaféProfitCalc.com

Written by Terry McDonnellChartered accountant; built, ran and sold seven fast food franchises; owned and operated a restaurant for nine years.

Coffee is the best margin product in a café. A well-made flat white costs about $1.00–1.50 to produce once you count beans, milk, milk waste, the cup and lid, and the share of customers on oat milk, and it sells for $5–6. That's a cost percentage of roughly 18–25%, still better than almost any food item on your menu. But many café operators don't actually know their coffee cost percentage, because they've never calculated it properly.

What should your coffee cost percentage be?

Total coffee and beverage cost should sit at 22–30% of beverage revenue. The lower end is achievable for high-volume operations with efficient milk usage and well-priced menus. The upper end is typical for specialty cafés with premium beans and higher milk ratios.

Above 32%? You likely have a milk waste problem, underpriced drinks, or bean costs that have risen without a menu price adjustment.

What's actually in your coffee cost

Coffee beans

A double shot espresso uses approximately 18–22g of ground coffee. At $30–50 per kg for specialty beans, that's $0.54–$1.10 per double shot. Many operators only count beans when calculating coffee cost, which significantly understates the real number.

Milk

Beans are usually the biggest single cost in a milk coffee, but milk is the one that moves most — because it's where the waste happens. A flat white or latte uses 150–200ml of milk. At $1.50–2.00 per litre, that's $0.23–0.40 per drink — before accounting for any waste or oversteaming. Across hundreds of coffees per day, milk cost is significant and worth tracking separately.

Cups and consumables

Takeaway cups, lids, sleeves, and stirrers add $0.15–0.30 per takeaway coffee. For a café doing 200 takeaway coffees per day, that's $30–60 per day in consumables alone — over $10,000–$20,000 per year.

Syrups and extras

Sugar-free syrups, vanilla, caramel, alternative milks — these add cost that is often not tracked. Oat milk at $2.50–3.50 per litre vs dairy at $1.50 is a meaningful cost difference if 20–30% of your customers choose it.

How to calculate coffee cost per cup

Work it out one drink at a time. Here's a takeaway flat white:

IngredientCalculationCost
Beans20g at $40/kg$0.80
Milk180ml at $1.80/litre$0.32
Milk waste15% on top of milk used$0.05
Cup and lidTakeaway only$0.20
Total$1.37

At a menu price of $5.50, that's a coffee cost of $1.37 ÷ $5.50 = 24.9%.

Use your price before tax. If your $5.50 includes sales tax, GST or VAT, that part isn't yours. With 15% tax included, the real selling price is $4.78 — and the same coffee costs 28.6%, not 24.9%. Many operators quietly overstate their margin this way.

How the cost varies by drink

Your coffee cost percentage is an average across very different drinks. Using the same prices as above:

A shift in your mix — more takeaway, more alternative milks — can push your overall percentage up even when nothing in your recipes has changed. That's why an alternative-milk surcharge that reflects the real cost matters. For pricing the rest of your menu, see how to price your café menu for profit.

The milk waste problem

Milk waste is the most common reason coffee cost runs high. Oversteaming, incorrect ratios, split pitchers, and milk poured and not used all add up. A barista who consistently oversteams and tips 50ml per drink is wasting roughly 10 litres of milk per 200 coffees served — about $15–20 per day, or $5,000–7,000 per year from one person's habit.

The solution is barista training, standardised ratios, and measuring actual milk usage against theoretical usage. Cafés doing this for the first time are often surprised by how big the gap is — and closing it has a direct impact on beverage cost percentage.

Checking theoretical against actual

The same check works for beans. Take the coffees sold from your POS and multiply by your dose to get what you should have used, then compare it with what you actually bought:

1,400 coffees a week × 20g = 28kg of beans expected
32kg actually bought ÷ 28kg = 14% more than expected

Some gap is normal — dialling in the grinder each morning, purging, the odd remade drink. But staff coffees, free drinks for friends and heavy-handed dosing all hide in that gap too. Track it monthly; a regular stocktake gives you the "actually used" figure.

When did you last raise your coffee price?

Many roasters have put their prices up sharply in recent years as green coffee costs have risen. If your flat white is still $5.00 and your bean cost has risen from $28/kg to $42/kg, your coffee cost percentage has shifted significantly without you changing anything. At 20g a double shot, that's an extra $0.28 a cup. A $0.50 price increase on your standard coffee — from $5.00 to $5.50 — more than covers it. Most customers accept small price increases without complaint if the quality justifies it.

Cost percentage vs dollars per cup

A low percentage is a good sign, but it's dollars that pay the rent. A $5.00 coffee at 25% cost leaves $3.75. A $6.00 coffee at 27% cost leaves $4.38 — a slightly worse percentage, but 63 cents more profit on every cup. When you review prices, look at both numbers.

How to bring your coffee cost down

  • Standardise your recipes. Fix the dose, the yield and the milk volume for every drink, and make sure every barista works to them.
  • Use the right jug for the drink. Steaming in a jug sized for the cup cuts the milk left over at the end of each pour.
  • Train against waste. Show your team the dollar cost of tipped milk and remade drinks. Most baristas have never been told.
  • Review your supplier pricing. Ask your roaster about volume pricing or a fixed-price agreement, and compare it against what you're paying now.
  • Price alternative milks properly. Set any surcharge from the real extra cost, not what the café down the road charges.
  • Raise prices in step with costs. Small, regular increases are easier for customers to accept than one large jump after years of absorbing costs.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good coffee cost percentage?

A total beverage cost of 22–30% of beverage revenue is a common target. Busy cafés with tight milk control sit at the lower end; specialty cafés with premium beans tend to sit higher. Above 32% usually points to milk waste, underpriced drinks or bean costs that have risen without a price change.

How much does a cup of coffee cost a café to make?

A milk coffee typically costs around $1.00–1.50 once you include beans, milk, milk waste and a takeaway cup. A black coffee served in a cup costs less, and alternative-milk drinks cost more.

How do I calculate coffee cost percentage?

Add up the cost of everything in the drink — beans, milk, waste, cup and lid — and divide by the menu price excluding sales tax, GST or VAT. Multiply by 100 to get a percentage.

Should I charge extra for oat milk?

If oat or other alternative milks cost noticeably more than dairy, a surcharge set from the real cost difference protects your margin. Without one, every alternative-milk drink earns less than your standard coffee.

Why is my coffee cost higher than it should be?

The usual causes are milk waste, recipes that aren't followed consistently, untracked staff and free drinks, and menu prices that haven't kept up with bean and milk costs.
