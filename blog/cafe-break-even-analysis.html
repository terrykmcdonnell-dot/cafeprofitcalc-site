Café Break-Even Analysis: How to Calculate Your Break-Even Point

Published April 2026  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  7 min read  ·  CaféProfitCalc.com

Written by Terry McDonnellChartered accountant; built, ran and sold seven fast food franchises; owned and operated a restaurant for nine years.

Every café has a break-even point — the exact weekly revenue needed to cover all costs and generate precisely zero profit. Below that number you're losing money, above it you're making money. Knowing your break-even point is fundamental to understanding whether your café is viable, particularly when evaluating a new location, modelling a quiet season, or deciding whether a rent increase is survivable.

Fixed costs vs variable costs

Fixed costs don't change with revenue: rent, loan repayments, insurance, management salaries, software subscriptions. You pay these whether you serve 50 customers or 300.

Variable costs scale with revenue: coffee and food cost, hourly wages, packaging, card processing fees. The more you sell, the more these cost.

The grey areas

Some costs don't sit neatly in either box, and how you treat them changes your answer:

  • Labour is both. The minimum crew you need to open the doors, salaried staff and your own wage are fixed. Extra casual hours rostered for busy periods are variable. Split the wage bill rather than forcing it all into one side.
  • Utilities are mostly fixed. Power and gas rise a little on busy days, but most of the bill comes from keeping the machine, fridges and lights running. Treat them as fixed unless your bills swing a lot.
  • Your own salary is fixed — and it must be included. Pay yourself a market-rate wage in the calculation, even if you don't draw it. More on why below.

Break-even formula:
Break-even revenue = Fixed costs ÷ (1 − variable cost %)

Example: $3,500 weekly fixed costs ÷ (1 − 0.65) = $10,000 weekly break-even revenue

Step by step

Step 1: Calculate your weekly fixed costs

List every cost that doesn't change with revenue, then convert monthly figures to weekly by dividing by 4.33 (not 4 — there are 52 weeks in a year, not 48). For a typical suburban café open seven days:

Fixed costPer month
Rent and outgoings$3,500
Owner/manager salary at market rate$5,600
Loan or equipment lease repayments$1,500
Power, gas and water$900
Accounting, POS and software subscriptions$400
Insurance$300
Repairs, cleaning and other$300
Total$12,500

$12,500 ÷ 4.33 = roughly $2,887 a week in fixed costs.

Step 2: Calculate your variable cost percentage

Add your food and beverage cost percentage to your variable labour percentage (hourly staff only, not salaried management). If food/bev cost is 30% and variable labour is 32%, your variable cost percentage is 62%. (Our guide to café labour cost covers how to split and control the labour line.)

Step 3: Apply the formula

$2,887 weekly fixed costs ÷ (1 − 0.62) = $7,597 weekly break-even revenue — call it $7,600. Below that you're losing money; above it, 38 cents of every extra dollar of sales is profit.

Step 4: Turn it into customers per day

A weekly dollar figure is hard to picture behind the counter. Divide it by your trading days, then by your average spend per customer:

$7,600 ÷ 7 days = $1,085 a day
$1,085 ÷ $7 average spend = about 155 customers a day

That's a number you can test against reality: count your customers on a normal Tuesday and see how close you are. It also shows why raising your average spend matters so much — at $8 a head, the same café needs about 136 customers a day. See how to increase café revenue without more customers.

Step 5: Work out your margin of safety

Your margin of safety is how far sales can fall before you start losing money:

Margin of safety = (Actual sales − Break-even sales) ÷ Actual sales

If this café trades at $9,500 a week, its margin of safety is ($9,500 − $7,600) ÷ $9,500 = 20%. Sales can drop by a fifth before the business stops covering its costs. Under 10% is a café with almost no room for a wet month, a new competitor or a broken machine.

Using break-even before signing a lease

This is where break-even analysis is most valuable. Before committing to a location, calculate your break-even point based on the projected rent and realistic operating costs. Then ask: how many coffees per day at my target average transaction value does it take to hit that break-even? Is that number achievable in that location?

A café paying $5,000/month rent with a break-even of $18,000/week needs to serve roughly 350–400 customers per day at $6–7 average spend to break even — every week, including the quiet ones. If the location can't support that volume, the rent is too high regardless of how beautiful the fit-out is. Our guide to negotiating a café lease covers what to push for before you sign.

What moves your break-even point

Break-even is most useful for testing decisions before you make them. Using the same café (fixed costs $2,887 a week, variable costs 62%, break-even $7,600):

ChangeNew break-evenDifference
Rent rises $500 a month$7,901+$304 a week
Food and labour creep up 2 points (to 64%)$8,019+$422 a week
Tighter costs bring variable costs down 2 points (to 60%)$7,217−$380 a week
Owner's salary left out$4,194Understated by $3,403 a week

Two things stand out. A two-point drift in food and labour costs hurts more than a sizeable rent rise — which is why weekly coffee cost and labour cost checks matter. And leaving out the owner's wage makes the café look viable at barely half the sales it really needs.

Common break-even mistakes

  • Leaving out your own wage. As the table shows, it's the single biggest distortion. A café that only breaks even because the owner works for nothing isn't breaking even.
  • Treating all labour as variable. If you put your entire wage bill into variable costs, you'll understate fixed costs and get a break-even that's too low.
  • Dividing monthly costs by four. Use 4.33. Dividing by four overstates weekly costs by about 8%.
  • Forgetting card fees and packaging. Both rise with every sale and belong in variable costs. In a takeaway-heavy café they add up quickly.
  • Using one average week. If your trade is seasonal, compare break-even against each month, not the annual average. That's where the next section comes in.

Break-even for quiet season planning

Once you know your break-even point, you can model your quiet months realistically. If your break-even is $9,000/week and your quietest month averages $7,500/week, you know you'll lose approximately $1,500/week for those 4–5 weeks — about $6,000–7,500 in total. That's cash you need to have in reserve before the quiet season arrives, not after it ends.

Covering that gap is a cash flow problem as much as a profit problem — a café can be profitable across the year and still run short of cash in its quietest months.

Frequently asked questions

What is a break-even point for a café?

It's the weekly or monthly sales figure at which your café covers all its costs and makes exactly zero profit. Above it you make money; below it you lose money.

How do I calculate my café's break-even point?

Add up your weekly fixed costs, then divide by one minus your variable cost percentage. With $2,887 of weekly fixed costs and variable costs at 62%, break-even is $2,887 ÷ 0.38 = about $7,600 a week.

Should I include my own salary in break-even?

Yes. Include a market-rate wage for the work you do, even if you don't pay it to yourself. Leaving it out can make break-even look far lower than it really is.

How many customers does a café need to break even?

Divide your daily break-even sales by your average spend per customer. A café needing $1,085 a day at a $7 average spend needs about 155 customers a day.

What is a good margin of safety for a café?

A margin of safety of 20% or more gives you room to absorb a quiet month or an unexpected cost. Under 10% leaves very little buffer.
